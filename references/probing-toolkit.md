# Probing Toolkit

Use this toolkit to choose the next question after an interviewee has answered. Keep the method invisible: select one relevant probe, ask it naturally, and listen to the result. The interview's purpose and main thread take precedence over completing any taxonomy.

## Quick routing

| Signal in the latest answer | Preferred next move |
|---|---|
| Generic claim or repeated habit | Ask for one specific, time-framed example |
| Event lacks scene, sequence, action, or internal response | Ask for descriptive detail |
| Important word is vague, loaded, or possibly ambiguous | Echo the exact word and clarify it |
| Event and terms are clear, but the person's perceived reasoning is missing | Ask for their explanation or decision process |
| Concrete, complex professional judgment or critical incident | Use selective CTA/CDM decision probes |
| Answer is still unfolding | Leave space or echo a key phrase instead of adding a new frame |
| A broad interview is converging on one cause or a proposed action while key context is missing | Apply the parent skill's breadth check; park the provisional finding and ask about one consequential gap |
| Answer repeats, becomes speculative, or leaves the main question | Stop drilling, synthesize briefly, and return to the conversation map |

Do not ask several rows at once. Choose the question most likely to produce new, relevant evidence.

## DICE as a probe-selection map

DICE is a set of four probe types, not a mandatory four-step script.

### Descriptive detail

Use when an episode is present but underspecified. Recover both the outer scene and the user's inner experience.

- Outer: What happened, who was involved, what came before, and what happened next?
- Inner: What did the user notice, think, feel, want, or expect at that moment?

Prefer one missing dimension at a time. Do not turn the interview into an exhaustive incident form.

### Idiographic memory

Use when the user speaks in averages or abstractions such as “I always,” “people usually,” or “generally.” Move from the generic claim to a particular event or time window.

Examples:

- “You said this happens often. What is one recent time it happened?”
- “Take me back to the last report where you noticed that.”

A specific memory is evidence for understanding the pattern, not automatic proof that the pattern is universal.

### Clarifying meaning

Use when a consequential word could conceal several meanings. Begin with the interviewee's own language.

Examples:

- “You said the project was ‘失控’. What did ‘失控’ look like at that point?”
- “When you say ‘没有效果’, what result was missing?”

Clarification should reveal implicit meaning, not invite the interviewer to substitute a polished definition.

### Explanatory probing

Use after the event and key terms are clear, when the user's perceived reasons matter to the interview.

Examples:

- “What made you interpret it that way at the time?”
- “How did you arrive at that decision?”

Frame explanations as the user's attribution, not an objective cause. Avoid repeatedly asking “why”: it can produce defensive, over-intellectualized, or hindsight-shaped answers. Return to concrete evidence when an explanation becomes too smooth.

Descriptive and idiographic probes are often useful earlier; clarifying and explanatory probes may require more context and trust. This is a tendency, not a fixed order.

## Laddered probing

Laddering means probing the answer to the previous probe while it still contains an unresolved, relevant signal. It does not mean asking “why” until an abstract value appears.

Use this loop:

1. Identify one phrase, event, or judgment in the latest answer that matters to the main question.
2. Choose the lightest probe that can make it more concrete or precise.
3. Listen for genuinely new information.
4. Probe the new answer again only if it opens a relevant layer.
5. Summarize the emerging point before switching branches.

Stop laddering when:

- a concrete event and the key judgment process are understood;
- the interviewee repeats the same idea in new words;
- recall gives way to unsupported speculation;
- the user signals fatigue, discomfort, or inability to answer;
- the branch no longer advances the main interview purpose;
- a forced choice or interpretation would now produce more value than another depth probe.

## Critical decision deepening

Use this only for a specific complex task, professional judgment, or critical incident. Do not apply it mechanically to ordinary preferences or every life story.

First reconstruct a short timeline and identify the decision or turning point. Then select among:

- **Cues and information:** What first signaled that something was wrong or important? What information mattered most or was missing?
- **Meaning:** What did that signal mean to the user at the time?
- **Expectations:** What did they expect would happen next?
- **Goals:** What outcome or priority were they protecting? Were goals competing?
- **Options:** What alternatives did they consider? Why were others rejected?
- **Uncertainty:** What was unclear, risky, or difficult to judge?
- **Experience and rules:** Did a prior case, training, operating rule, or workaround shape the choice?
- **Counterfactual:** If the key cue or constraint had been absent, would they have acted differently?

Elicit before evaluating. Once the decision process is clear, return to the parent skill's normal behavior: test interpretations, introduce counterevidence, and make a provisional judgment when warranted. Critical Decision Method is a lens for expert cognition, not a ban on later critical thinking.

## Clean first probe

The first probe into a newly introduced experience should usually minimize interviewer-added content:

- reuse the user's consequential word or phrase;
- do not insert an unmentioned cause, emotion, motive, or solution;
- ask about what the user experienced rather than what the interviewer assumes happened.

Prefer:

> “You said ‘失控’. What kind of ‘失控’ was it?”

Avoid:

> “Was it失控 because the team communicated badly?”

This is a temporary elicitation discipline, not strict Clean Language interviewing. After the user's meaning is clear, the interviewer may paraphrase, interpret, challenge, compare, and state a judgment—while marking those additions as the interviewer's inference.

## Basic facilitation moves

- **Leave space:** In text, do not stack extra questions merely because the user pauses or says they need to think.
- **Echo:** Repeat one consequential phrase with a light invitation to continue.
- **Expansion:** Ask for more description when the answer is relevant but thin.
- **Example:** Move a general statement into a concrete occurrence.
- **Clarification:** Ask what the user's word means in that context.
- **Process:** Ask how the user noticed, inferred, chose, or changed their view.

These are facilitation moves, not evidence that an interview is deep. Their value is measured by the new, relevant detail they elicit.

## Quality check before asking

Ask silently:

1. Does this question follow from the user's latest words?
2. Does it advance the user's intended interview scope, or only deepen the current branch? If key context is missing, apply the parent skill's breadth check.
3. Am I requesting recall, meaning, or reasoning—and is that the right level now?
4. Have I inserted a cause, emotion, or framework the user did not provide?
5. Is one question enough?
6. What answer would tell me to stop probing and synthesize?

## Method boundaries and sources

- DICE and laddered probes: Oliver C. Robinson, “Probing in qualitative research interviews: Theory and practice” (2023), and Bob Price, “Laddered questions and qualitative data research interviews” (2002).
- Critical decision deepening: Olivia Brown, Nicola Power, and Julie Gore, “Cognitive Task Analysis: Eliciting Expert Cognition in Context” (published online 2024).
- Clean-language constraint: Jan Nehyba and James Lawley, “Clean Language Interviewing as a Second-Person Method in the Science of Consciousness” (2020).
- Basic facilitation moves: Melissa DeJonckheere and Lisa M. Vaughn, “Semistructured interviewing in primary care research: a balance of relationship and rigour” (2019).

Do not import Motivational Interviewing into this toolkit. Its behavior-change and counseling aims are outside the current scope.
